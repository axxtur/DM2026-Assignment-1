## 2. Preliminary Analysis (10%)
Before arriving at our final LightGBM approach, we conducted extensive preliminary analyses using Deep Learning architectures, specifically 1D Convolutional Neural Networks combined with LSTMs. We observed that the raw accelerometer sensor data was extremely noisy and exhibited high variability across different users. These deep learning approaches proved highly prone to overfitting on the specific movement patterns of the users present in the training set and failed to generalize well to unseen users, which consistently resulted in lower macro F1-scores. In contrast, preliminary tests utilizing tree-based gradient boosting models, specifically LightGBM, demonstrated vastly superior generalization when provided with robust, manually engineered statistical features. This critical observation led to our core design decision to pivot away from Deep Learning and entirely rely on a Gradient Boosting framework powered by heavy feature engineering.

## 3. Preprocessing Techniques (10%)
Our preprocessing pipeline largely eschewed raw signal scaling in favor of extensive statistical feature engineering, which yielded the most significant improvements in model performance. To prevent data leakage, we partitioned the training data using GroupKFold based on user IDs, ensuring that data from the same user never appeared in both the training and validation sets. This provided a highly reliable local cross-validation score that correctly estimated generalization capabilities. For feature engineering, we extracted a wide array of statistics across both time and frequency domains. In the time domain, we calculated the mean, standard deviation, minimum, maximum, median, quartiles, skewness, kurtosis, range, and the sum of squares to represent energy. We also computed the mean absolute difference and the standard deviation of differences to capture sudden jerky movements. In the frequency domain, we utilized the Fast Fourier Transform to extract the mean, standard deviation, and maximum values, successfully capturing rhythmic movement patterns such as walking. Furthermore, we calculated the Pearson correlation between the X, Y, and Z axes, alongside the overall Euclidean magnitude of acceleration. The inclusion of these engineered features provided a massive F1-score jump compared to naive baselines that merely relied on global averages.

## 4. Alignment of Activity Labels with Sequential Readings (10%)
The dataset provides only a single activity label for every 5-minute CSV file, meaning 300 seconds of sequential readings must be mapped to one target. Directly utilizing standard Machine Learning models on 300 raw sequential points is highly ineffective without recurrent structures. To properly align the sequential accelerometer readings with the single overarching label, we transformed the temporal sequence into a static feature vector utilizing a sub-windowing technique. We first computed our statistical features over the entire 300-second sequence to capture the global activity trend. Subsequently, we divided the 300-second window into five smaller, sequential blocks of 60 seconds each. We then computed the exact same statistical features for each of these 60-second blocks independently and concatenated them with the global features. This temporal alignment strategy allowed our LightGBM model to understand the general trend of the entire 5-minute window while simultaneously recognizing localized, transient temporal changes over the five distinct minute-intervals, thereby capturing essential temporal dependencies without the need for a complex LSTM.

## 5. Ablation Study for Core Technical Design Choices (10%)
Our experimental journey involved testing several core design choices, which ultimately proved that our 04_baseline_model was the most effective solution. As previously mentioned, an ablation replacing LightGBM with CNN and LSTM architectures resulted in a severe drop in the macro F1-score, proving that LightGBM with engineered features was far superior for this specific tabularized sequential data. We also observed that relying solely on the 300-second global features led to a substantial decrease in performance because the global features averaged out too much critical localized information, proving that the 60-second sub-windows were absolutely essential. To push performance further, we experimented with increasing the sub-window granularity to 10 blocks of 30 seconds and adding an even larger number of features in our subsequent 06_grandmaster_model. Counterintuitively, this highly granular approach actually decreased performance due to the excessive feature count causing LightGBM to overfit the training data. We additionally attempted an 05_ensemble_model approach combining various predictions, but this too performed worse than the standalone baseline_model. Ultimately, the 04_baseline_model, utilizing five 60-second sub-windows and a feature subsampling rate of 80 percent, represented the perfect sweet spot, successfully avoiding the overfitting pitfalls that plagued both the grandmaster and ensemble models.
